## <a id="_Toc184710002"></a>Chapter 2: The European education and qualifications landscape

The European education and qualifications landscape presents a complex and diverse picture, characterised by varied approaches across member states. As digital technologies advance, this sector faces significant challenges and opportunities for transformation. 

This chapter explores the complex landscape of education and qualifications across EU Member States, highlighting the decentralised authority that shapes each country's approach to credential issuance, verification, and recognition. By identifying the varied administrative processes, regulatory frameworks, and licensing requirements, this chapter underscores the challenges and opportunities for implementing a unified digital credential system. Understanding these diverse structures is crucial for developing solutions that respect national autonomy while facilitating cross-border educational and professional mobility.

### <a id="_Toc182376656"></a><a id="_Toc184710003"></a>2.1 Decentralised authority and Member State autonomy

One of the defining features of the European education system is its balanced approach between national autonomy and coordinated standards. The Bologna Process, initiated in 1999, established a crucial framework for harmonizing higher education systems while respecting member states' educational sovereignty.

#### Bologna Process achievements:

The Bologna Process created the European Higher Education Area (EHEA), implementing key standardisation tools:

- **Three-cycle system**: Bachelor's/Master's/Doctorate structure across participating countries
- **ECTS**: European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System for credit recognition
- **Diploma Supplement**: Standardised information about qualification content and context  
- **Quality assurance standards**: Shared frameworks for maintaining educational quality

While education remains primarily under member state jurisdiction per the principle of subsidiarity, the Bologna Process provides a voluntary framework that has successfully standardized many aspects of higher education. This achievement demonstrates how European cooperation can respect national autonomy while creating effective common standards.

#### Digital credentials adaptation needs:

Unfortunately, neither the Bologna Process nor the Lisbon Recognition Convention provides specific rules dedicated to digital credentials. However, both frameworks create a foundation that can be adapted to incorporate and support the recognition and use of digital credentials within the European Higher Education Area (EHEA).

The absence of a single European-level body governing education standards has led to a rich variety of educational approaches across the continent. Each member state maintains its own system for curriculum design, assessment methods, and qualification recognition. This autonomy allows countries to tailor their educational systems to their specific cultural, economic, and social needs.

**Example**: The German dual education system, which combines apprenticeships in a company with vocational education at a vocational school, reflects the country's strong emphasis on practical skills and industry involvement in education.

However, this autonomy also presents challenges for cross-border recognition of qualifications and student mobility. A degree or qualification that is well-understood and valued in one country may not be easily recognised or appreciated in another. This can create barriers for students wishing to study abroad or professionals seeking employment in different EU countries.

The operational model addressing these varying requirements is detailed in [Chapter 4](chapter4.md), with specific technical solutions presented in [Section 7.3](chapter7.md#73-model-structure).

### <a id="_Toc182376657"></a><a id="_Toc184710004"></a>2.2 Regulated education levels and licensing requirements

Analysis of 17 DC4EU participant countries reveals significant variation in licensing requirements across educational sectors:

| Education Level | Licensing Requirement Rate | Notes |
|----------------|---------------------------|-------|
| **Universities** | 100% | All surveyed countries require licensing |
| **Universities of Applied Sciences** | 88% | High regulation in vocational higher education |
| **General Upper Secondary Education** | 88% | Strong regulatory oversight for secondary qualifications |
| **Technical and Vocational Education** | 82% | Substantial regulation in skills-based education |
| **Primary Education** | 81% | High oversight for foundational education |
| **Liberal Adult Education** | 46% | Lower regulation due to broader scope and non-formal structure |

These figures demonstrate high regulation levels in formal education sectors, with liberal adult education showing lower regulation rates due to its broader scope and non-formal structure.

### <a id="_Toc182376658"></a><a id="_Toc184710005"></a>2.3 Approaches to licensing educational institutions

Educational institution licensing follows three primary models across member states:

#### Licensing model comparison:

| Model Type | Description | Examples | Administrative Structure |
|------------|-------------|----------|-------------------------|
| **Centralised** | Ministry-level institutions manage licensing, with authority distributed among departments by education level | Most common approach among surveyed countries | Unitary states typically employ this system |
| **Regional** | Federal states assign licensing authority to regional governments | Germany - each region maintains independent educational licensing systems under national guidelines | Federal states typically employ regional systems |
| **Mixed** | Combines central and regional authority | Sweden - municipalities manage school licensing through secondary level, whilst national body licenses higher education | Balances local control with national oversight |

These models align with established national administrative structures, with federal states typically employing regional systems and unitary states maintaining centralised licensing.

### <a id="_Toc182376659"></a><a id="_Toc184710006"></a>2.4 Administrative vs legislative approaches

Educational licensing combines administrative and legislative processes with significant national variation:

#### Legislative vs administrative approaches:

**Legislative establishment approach:**
- **Finland, Portugal and Romania**: Use specific legislation to establish universities
- Provides strong legal foundation but requires parliamentary processes for changes

**Administrative framework approach:**  
- **National frameworks**: Grant autonomy to local authorities for primary education oversight
- Enables flexible implementation while maintaining national standards

#### License duration and renewal:

**Permanent licensing model:**
- Several countries issue permanent licenses with ongoing compliance monitoring
- Provides stability but requires robust oversight mechanisms

**Periodic review model:**
- Other states mandate periodic reviews (e.g., Romania's five-year evaluation cycle for pre-university institutions)
- Ensures continued quality but increases administrative burden

These variations in licence validity periods affect credential verification procedures, requiring adaptable monitoring systems for European digital credential systems.

### <a id="_Toc182376660"></a><a id="_Toc184710007"></a>2.5 Lifecycle of licenses

The duration and renewal processes for educational licenses vary considerably across Europe:

#### License validity approaches:

**Indefinite validity model:**
- Many countries grant licenses with indefinite validity, subject to continued compliance with requirements
- Provides institutional stability and reduces administrative burden
- Requires effective ongoing monitoring mechanisms

**Periodic evaluation model:**
- Some countries implement regular evaluation cycles
- **Example**: Romanian pre-university education institutions undergo evaluation every 5 years
- Ensures regular quality assessment but increases administrative complexity

This diversity in license lifecycles presents challenges for creating a unified system of credential verification across Europe, as the status and validity of an institution's license may need to be checked at different intervals depending on the country.

### <a id="_Toc182376661"></a><a id="_Toc184710008"></a>2.6 Information management on licensed institutions

Licensed institution data management follows distinct patterns with significant implications for European digital systems:

| Management Pattern | Percentage | Description | Implementation |
|-------------------|------------|-------------|----------------|
| **Central management** | 70% | National ministries maintain records for all education levels | Single point of truth, consistent standards |
| **Split management** | 24% | Central authorities oversee higher education data, with regional bodies managing pre-higher education records | Balanced approach, sector-specific expertise |  
| **Regional management** | 6% | Local authorities control all institutional data | Maximum local autonomy, potential inconsistency |

This distribution of information management approaches has significant implications for the development of a European-wide digital credential system. A system would need to interface with various national and regional databases, each potentially structured differently and operating under different regulatory frameworks.

### <a id="_Toc182376662"></a><a id="_Toc184710009"></a>2.7 Electronic Diploma issuance

Electronic diploma adoption across Europe shows four distinct implementation patterns:

| Implementation Pattern | Percentage | Countries | Characteristics |
|------------------------|------------|-----------|----------------|
| **Full implementation** | 33% | Denmark, Greece, Lithuania, Netherlands, Norway, Portugal | Electronic certificates as primary credentials for selected education levels |
| **Permissive framework** | 17% | Finland, Sweden | Institutions can select certificate formats through non-restrictive legislation |
| **Active transition** | 28% | Estonia, Latvia, Poland, Romania, selected German states | Developing electronic certificate systems through legal reforms and national digital storage services |
| **Paper requirement** | 22% | Cyprus, Hungary, Italy, Spain | Legal requirements maintain paper certificates as primary format |

This mixed electronic and paper-based environment requires systems supporting both formats, highlighting the need for flexible digital credential solutions that can accommodate varying national approaches.

### <a id="_Toc182376666"></a><a id="_Toc184710013"></a>2.11 Limitations of current solutions

While various initiatives have been launched to address challenges in the European education landscape, many solutions have significant limitations:

#### Current system limitations:

**Design priorities:**
- **Institution-focused design**: Many current solutions prioritise the needs of educational institutions and employers over those of students and graduates
- **Limited user control**: Students have minimal control over their own educational data

**Technical limitations:**
- **Inadequate revocation mechanisms**: Popular platforms like Europass EDCI lack effective methods for revoking or updating qualifications
- **Federation constraints**: National federations and systems like eduGAIN provide authentication services but not comprehensive qualification verification and portability
- **Interoperability issues**: Many existing solutions struggle to work seamlessly across different member states or sectors

**Scalability concerns:**
- **Limited scalability**: Current approaches often face difficulties in scaling to serve millions of users across Europe
- **Fragmented implementation**: Lack of coordinated approach across educational sectors and countries

### <a id="_Toc182376667"></a><a id="_Toc184710014"></a>2.12 New paradigm due to digitalisation

From a digitalisation perspective, the European education and qualifications landscape is characterised by diversity, autonomy, and historical separation from an organisational perspective (different processes, different credentials, different schemes). While this diversity allows for tailored approaches to education in different countries, it also presents significant challenges for cross-border recognition of qualifications and student mobility.

#### Digital transformation opportunities:

**Current state analysis:**
The European education system balances member state autonomy with cross-border coordination needs. Digital solutions must accommodate national education frameworks while supporting qualification recognition and student mobility.

**Digital wallet potential:**
Digital wallets could provide students and professionals with a secure, portable means of storing and sharing their educational credentials.

#### Digital solutions for current limitations:

**Technical capabilities:**
- **Secure credential storage**: EUDI wallets providing tamper-proof, portable storage
- **Verifiable records**: Distributed ledger technologies ensuring credential authenticity
- **Standardised data exchange**: Common formats enabling cross-border recognition

**Implementation requirements:**
- **Common data standards**: Unified approaches to credential data representation
- **Privacy protection measures**: GDPR-compliant systems protecting individual rights
- **Cross-border interoperability protocols**: Systems enabling seamless European mobility

#### Implementation challenges:

However, the successful implementation of these technologies would require addressing several challenges:

- **Widespread adoption**: Ensuring participation across diverse educational systems
- **Data privacy maintenance**: Balancing transparency with individual privacy rights  
- **Standardised formats**: Creating educational data formats usable across different systems and countries

As Europe moves forward, the challenge will be to harness digital opportunities while respecting the autonomy of member states and the rich diversity of educational traditions across the continent.
